Where do Australia's lost and surrendered pets go?

Of every 100 cats taken in by Victorian shelters and pounds in 2025, about 27 were euthanased. For dogs, it was about 10.

By [Your name]. FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2, Monash University. October 2026.

Every year, tens of thousands of dogs and cats arrive at shelters and pounds. Some are strays who go home. Some are adopted by new owners. Some are euthanased.

This page uses two data sets to follow them: the RSPCA's national statistics, which show each state, and the Victorian Government's report on every shelter and pound in Victoria, which shows what happens in individual facilities. It starts with Australia and ends in Victoria.

Words used on this page

Received
An animal taken in by a shelter or pound. Also called admitted.
Returned
The animal goes back to its owner. The data also calls this reclaimed.
Rehomed
The animal is adopted by a new owner.
Transferred
The animal is moved to another organisation to be rehomed.
Euthanased
The animal is put to sleep by a vet.

The RSPCA across Australia

National figures for the 2024-25 financial year, state by state. They come from the RSPCA's own shelters, not from every pound in the country.

Queensland takes in more than half of the RSPCA's animals

Start with scale. In 2024-25 the RSPCA in Queensland received 43,041 animals of all kinds. That is 56% of the national total of 77,464. The Northern Territory came second with 12,410.

Read with careThese bars show what each state's RSPCA reports, not how many animals the state has. The ACT, NT and Tasmania leave out pound animals, and New South Wales reports only 465.

In every state, at least half of the dogs and cats received were rehomed

From here the page looks at dogs and cats only. The share rehomed runs from 50% in Western Australia to 69% in the ACT. The two biggest circles, Queensland and the Northern Territory, sit near the middle of that range.

How to readCircle area is the number of dogs and cats received. Darker blue means a larger share was rehomed.

NoticeQueensland (55%) and the NT (52%) receive the most animals but rehome a smaller share than the ACT (69%).

The Northern Territory euthanases the largest share

Rehoming is not the whole story. In the Northern Territory, 27% of dogs and cats received were euthanased. That is more than five times the share in New South Wales (5%). Queensland (20%), South Australia (18%) and Western Australia (17%) are also high.

How to readEach tile is one state, placed roughly where it sits on the map. Tiles are all the same size, so small states like the ACT are as easy to see as large ones.

Read with careNew South Wales reports very few animals, so its low share may not describe the whole state.

Queensland and the Northern Territory set the national picture

This chart joins the last three. Each column is a state. Its width is the state's share of all dogs and cats received, and its height shows what happened to them. Queensland (35%) and the Northern Territory (24%) take up 59% of the width, so their outcomes count most.

How to readWide column: many animals. Orange section: euthanased. Grey section: still in care or another outcome.

NoticeThe thin column on the right is New South Wales, 1% of the total.

Only Queensland and Victoria return about one animal in six to its owner

Going back to an owner is much less common than being rehomed. The share runs from 4% in New South Wales to 17% in Queensland, with Victoria close behind on 16%.

How to readDarker green means a larger share went back to their owners. Animals reclaimed by an owner and animals returned to an owner are counted together.

Read with careThe ACT, NT and Tasmania leave out pound animals, which are the animals most likely to be claimed.

Five years on

Is the number of animals changing, and what else do RSPCA teams deal with?

The RSPCA now receives far fewer cats and dogs than in 2020-21

Between 2020-21 and 2024-25, cats received fell by 40% (from 42,572 to 25,639) and dogs by 29% (from 22,311 to 15,852). Other animals fell by only 6%.

How to readEach line joins one animal group between the two years. A steeper fall means a bigger drop.

Read with careThe data show that intake fell, not why. Changes in reporting or in RSPCA services could play a part.

Behind the shelters, thousands of cruelty complaints

RSPCA teams do more than run shelters. In 2024-25 they investigated 55,772 animal cruelty complaints across seven states and territories. New South Wales had the most (16,713), even though its shelter figures are the smallest.

How to readEach ring is one state. The longer the coloured arc, the more complaints. The grey track behind each arc shows the full scale.

Read with careThe Northern Territory is missing because RSPCA NT cannot enforce animal welfare law there.

Inside Victoria

Every Victorian shelter and pound must report what happened to its dogs and cats. For 2025 that is 68 facilities and 75,440 admissions, which makes it possible to compare one facility with another.

Councils and a few large shelters take in most of Victoria's animals

Council pounds took in about a third of all admissions (34%), and other shelters and rescue groups another third (34%). The two Lost Dogs Home sites handled a quarter (25%) on their own. The two RSPCA sites handled 7%.

How to readEach square is 1% of the 75,440 admissions. Squares of one colour belong to one type of facility.

Read with careThe groups are mine, based on facility names. Some councils pay another facility to run their pound, so council figures are not complete.

Dogs tend to go home. Cats tend to be rehomed or euthanased.

Nearly half of the dogs admitted (47%) were reclaimed by their owners, compared with only 6% of cats. Cats were more likely to be rehomed (43% against 26%), transferred (20% against 15%) or euthanased (27% against 10%).

How to readEach row is one outcome. The green dot is dogs and the orange dot is cats. The further right, the larger the share of animals admitted.

Read with careAdmissions count every arrival, so an animal admitted twice counts twice. The shares are close estimates.

Cats are often euthanased for being wild. Dogs, mostly for behaviour.

More than half of the cats euthanased (6,682 of 12,822) were unidentified, wild or uncontrollable cats, a reason that applies only to cats. For dogs, behaviour (1,148) and being unsuitable for sale (1,130) account for 78% of the 2,907 euthanased.

How to readDogs extend to the left and cats to the right from the centre line. Reasons are sorted from the largest total to the smallest.

Read with careThese are counts, not shares. Cats were admitted far more often (47,581 against 27,859), so longer cat bars partly reflect their numbers.

Where an animal ends up changes its odds

Among the 15 busiest facilities, the share euthanased runs from 5% at the Cat Protection Society of Victoria and the City of Ballarat Animal Shelter to 47% at the Mildura Rural City Council Pound. Both Lost Dogs Home sites are about a third (32% and 33%).

How to readEach row is a facility, from busiest at the top. Darker blue means a larger share of its animals had that outcome.

Read with careBrimbank and Wyndham transferred 99% and 72% of their animals to other organisations, so their euthanasia figures cannot show the full story.

Across Victoria, rates range from near zero to three in four

Each dot is one of the 55 facilities with at least 100 admissions. The statewide rate is 21%, and 35 of the 55 sit below 20%. A few small council pounds are far to the right, such as Hindmarsh Shire (75% of 193 admissions) and Strathbogie (65% of 240).

How to readEach dot is a facility. Dots further right euthanase a larger share of their admissions. Dots stack when facilities have similar rates.

Read with careSmall facilities swing widely on a handful of animals. Thirteen facilities with fewer than 100 admissions are left out.

What to take away

Three points from the two data sets.

  • Most animals leave care alive

    Across RSPCA states the share euthanased runs from 5% to 27%, and in Victoria it is 21% overall. Rehoming and going home are far more common.

  • Where and what the animal is both matter

    The same state has facilities at 5% and at 47%. Dogs are far more likely to go home, while cats are more likely to be rehomed or euthanased.

  • Counting differs, so compare with care

    Some RSPCA states leave out pounds, and Victorian facilities report events and animals in different ways. The charts show patterns, not exact league tables.

How the numbers were prepared

  • Dogs and cats are combined except where a chart separates them.
  • RSPCA outcomes were grouped: reclaimed and returned became returned to owner, and currently in care and other became still in care or other.
  • Victorian admissions and most outcomes count events, while euthanasia and deaths count unique animals. Shares of admissions are therefore approximate.
  • Facility types in Figure 8 come from facility names. The data set has no type column.
  • Victorian cat euthanasia includes the separate category for unidentified, wild or uncontrollable cats.

Limits to keep in mind

  • RSPCA figures cover RSPCA shelters, not every pound in a state. The ACT, NT and Tasmania leave out pound animals, and NSW reports few animals.
  • Not every Victorian council is listed, because some contract their pound to another facility. That facility's figures can include animals from outside the council shown.
  • The data show what happened to animals, not why. Nothing here proves that one facility is better or worse than another.
